A neural network is a computational model composed of layers of simple processing units called neurons, connected by weighted links. The design is loosely inspired by biological brains, though modern artificial neural networks work very differently from biological ones at a mechanical level.

The simplest neural network, called a perceptron, takes a set of numerical inputs, multiplies each by a weight, sums the results, and passes the sum through an activation function to produce an output. A single perceptron can only learn very simple, linearly separable patterns. Stacking many such units into layers, with nonlinear activation functions between them, allows a network to approximate much more complex functions, a property known as universal approximation.

A standard feedforward neural network consists of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. Information flows in one direction, from input to output, during prediction. During training, the network's predictions are compared to the correct answers using a loss function, and an optimization algorithm such as stochastic gradient descent adjusts the weights throughout the network to reduce this loss over many iterations.

Different neural network architectures are suited to different types of data. Convolutional neural networks are designed for grid-like data such as images. Recurrent neural networks and their variants, such as long short-term memory networks, are designed for sequential data such as time series or text, since they maintain an internal state that carries information from earlier steps in the sequence. Transformer networks, which rely on a mechanism called self-attention rather than recurrence, have become the dominant architecture for processing sequences, including in large language models.
