Natural language processing, often abbreviated NLP, is the field concerned with enabling computers to understand, interpret, and generate human language. Unlike structured data such as spreadsheets, language is ambiguous, context-dependent, and full of idioms, making it a difficult problem for computers to process directly.

A typical NLP pipeline begins with preprocessing steps such as tokenization, which splits text into individual words or subword units, and normalization steps such as lowercasing or removing punctuation. Once text has been broken into tokens, it must be converted into a numerical representation that a model can process, since machine learning models operate on numbers rather than raw text. Early approaches used simple counting methods such as bag-of-words or TF-IDF, which represent a document as a vector of word frequencies without capturing word order or meaning.

Word embeddings, such as those produced by Word2Vec or GloVe, improved on these count-based methods by learning dense vector representations where words with similar meanings are located close together in vector space. More recently, contextual embeddings produced by transformer-based models such as BERT assign a different vector to the same word depending on its surrounding context, allowing the model to distinguish between different meanings of the same word.

Common NLP tasks include sentiment analysis, which classifies text as expressing a positive, negative, or neutral opinion, named entity recognition, which identifies names of people, places, and organizations in text, machine translation, which converts text from one language to another, and information retrieval, which finds documents relevant to a given query. Retrieval-augmented generation systems combine an information retrieval step with a text generation model, first finding relevant passages from a document collection and then using a language model to generate an answer grounded in those passages.
